# Global reading UX research

Research for the warm-cream redesign, 2026-11-16. Every row says what was actually read. Items I could not read are listed at the end and are **not** used to support any decision.

**Evidence rule applied:** nothing here shows that cream, off-white or dark backgrounds improve eyesight, concentration, comprehension or eye health. Warm cream is the owner's stated preference (a design requirement, not a scientific conclusion). The studies below tested pure black/white or other conditions, not cream.

## Read but of no help for cream

None of sources 8–14 tested cream, off-white or sepia backgrounds. Source 12 does not mention them. **No evidence for or against cream was found.** Cream is kept at very high contrast (text 12.8:1 on the page colour) so that the preference does not cost legibility.

## Not read (not used as evidence)

Hall & Hanna 2004 (text/background colours), Rello et al. 2016 (font size and line spacing), Legge & Bigelow 2011 (print size), Dyson & Haselgrove 2001 and Shaikh 2005 (line length): the publisher pages returned HTTP 403 or no source was available. NN/g's line-length article URL returned 404. Line length therefore rests on WCAG 1.4.8 alone, plus rendered measurement. Cognitive-load and mobile-learning literature was not reviewed in depth; no decision depends on it.

## What the evidence supports, and what it does not

- Supported by measurement: dark-on-light text gave better proofreading performance than light-on-dark in lab tasks, more so at small sizes; this does not depend on room lighting (sources 8–11).
- Supported by standards: contrast, reflow, focus, text spacing, target size (sources 1–7).
- Not supported: that cream improves reading speed, comprehension or eye health; that dark mode reduces eye strain (source 11 found no difference in self-reported strain; source 12 says research is mixed).
- Preference, not evidence: warm cream; the sage-green accent; a green-gray charcoal dark theme.
